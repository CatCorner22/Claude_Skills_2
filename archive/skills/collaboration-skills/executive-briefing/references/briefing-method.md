## 1. The ask-calibration rail

Every briefing document declares which of three things it wants, near the top:

| Ask | What the reader does | The line that names it |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Nothing; awareness only | "No action requested." |
| **Decide** | Picks among presented options | "Decision requested: choose A, B, or C by <date>." |
| **Approve** | Signs or objects to a recommendation | "Approval requested: option B, by <date>." |

Rules of the rail:
- If the ask is unnamed, the document is read as *inform* — the lowest-energy reading —
  and nothing happens. This is the single most common briefing failure.
- *Decide* and *approve* both carry a by-when and a consequence-of-silence ("if we hear
  nothing by Friday, we hold the current process and the SLA misses continue").
- Asking *decide* when you have a recommendation is an abdication; asking *approve* when
  you genuinely need the reader's judgment among close options is presumption. Match the
  ask to the state of your staff work.
- One document, one ask. A memo requesting two decisions is two memos stapled together.

## 2. BLUF — the doctrine and its dating

DA Pam 600-67, *Effective Writing for Army Leaders* (issued 2 June 1986; rescinded
2013), set the Army staff-writing standard: main point at the beginning — the bottom
line up front — and prose a reader can grasp "in a single rapid reading" [snippet-only].
AR 25-50, *Preparing and Managing Correspondence* (first published 1988, revised since),
carries the two non-negotiables forward: main point first, active voice [snippet-only].

**The dating honesty point**: the *doctrine* is 1986; the *acronym* BLUF came later, and
the often-repeated claim pinning its first AR 25-50 appearance to a specific revision
rests on weak secondary sources. Safe formulation: "Army writing doctrine since the
1980s; the acronym came later." Do not date the acronym without checking the regulation
text itself [snippet-only, weak on the acronym-entry detail].

The BLUF itself is one or two sentences: the finding or recommendation plus the ask —
what you want, from whom, by when. Test: if the reader stops after the BLUF, do they
know what you need from them? If not, the bottom line is missing its bottom.

## 3. SCQA — the narrative entry

Barbara Minto's Situation–Complication–Question–Answer, from *The Minto Pyramid
Principle* (cite the 1996 edition; earlier edition dates are messy across sources)
[snippet-only]. Minto developed the method at McKinsey — she was the firm's first
female MBA hire and ran her own firm teaching it from 1973 — and it is hers by name;
"the McKinsey method" erases the named author [snippet-only].

| Element | Job | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | Ground the reader in what they already accept | Zero new claims; the reader nods |
| **Complication** | What changed, broke, or now threatens | Creates tension the reader feels |
| **Question** | The one question the complication forces | The reader would ask it themselves |
| **Answer** | Your bottom line | Identical in substance to the BLUF |

Two to four sentences total. SCQA is the anti-hot-take governor on the front end: it
proves the question is real before the answer claims attention. If you cannot write the
Complication without inventing urgency, the memo may not be needed — that finding is a
success of the method.

## 4. The pyramid under the answer

Minto's structural rule: the answer sits at the apex; beneath it, two to four grouped
reasons; beneath each reason, its evidence. Discipline, without the consulting cant:

- **Any line summarizes the lines below it.** A reader can stop at any level and hold a
  true (if compressed) version of the argument.
- **Groups don't overlap** — the same fact should not prop up two reasons; if it does,
  the reasons are one reason wearing two hats.
- **Groups don't leave an obvious gap** — the skeptical reader's first objection should
  land inside an existing group, not outside the structure. (This is the working meaning
  of Minto's mutually-exclusive, collectively-exhaustive grouping check — apply the
  check; you don't need the acronym.)
- **Order groups by what the reader needs**, usually strongest reason first — the reader
  may stop reading at any moment, so sequence for early exit.

The pyramid is why answer-first is safe: a reader who distrusts the BLUF descends one
level and meets the reasons; distrusting a reason, they descend to its evidence. Reading
becomes verification to the depth trust requires, instead of detective work.

## 5. The one-page decision memo template

```
TO: <decision-maker>          FROM: <owner>          DATE: <date>
RE: <one line — the decision, not the topic>

ASK: <INFORM / DECIDE / APPROVE> — <what, by when>

BOTTOM LINE: <recommendation or finding + ask, 1–2 sentences>

BACKGROUND (SCQA, 2–4 sentences):
  <Situation. Complication. Question — answered by the bottom line above.>

OPTIONS (each with its cost; do-nothing always present):
  A. Do nothing — <what it costs, by when the cost lands>
  B. <option> — <cost: money/hours/risk; what it buys>        ← RECOMMENDED
  C. <option> — <cost; why not recommended, one line>

RECOMMENDATION: <B, because — the top of the pyramid, 1–2 sentences>

IF NOTHING IS DECIDED BY <date>: <the specific consequence of silence>

SUPPORT: <2–4 grouped reasons, one line each, evidence attached or linked>
```

Anatomy rules:
- **Decision requested** appears twice — in the ASK line and inside the bottom line —
  because skimmers land in different places.
- **Options carry costs** in the reader's own currency (hours, money, risk, dates).
  An uncosted option is scenery.
- **Do-nothing is always an option** — it is what happens by default, and pricing it is
  what makes delay visible as a choice rather than a neutral state.
- **One page.** The support section links or appends the deep material; the memo is the
  apex and the first layer of the pyramid, not the whole structure.

## 6. The completed-staff-work test

The doctrine: "study a problem and present a solution in such form that all that
remains… is to indicate approval or disapproval of the completed action." Best
documented to Archer L. Lerch, then Deputy Provost Marshal General, published in the
*Army and Navy Journal*, January 1942; the memo also circulates credited to Brig.
G.E.R. Smith (First Canadian Army, 1943), and even friendly reprints concede the
original source is unclear — carry both names and the uncertainty [snippet-only].

The test, as a checklist:
- [ ] Could the reader sign (or refuse) and be done — no research left to them?
- [ ] Are the options already costed, or does the reader have to price them?
- [ ] Is there a recommendation, or is the hard call being handed upward?
- [ ] Is the consequence of inaction stated with a date?
- [ ] If the reader said only "yes" — could execution start tomorrow from this page?
- [ ] Have you removed every "happy to discuss further" that is actually "I stopped
      before the work was finished"?

A useful self-check from the doctrine's spirit: if you were the decision-maker, would
you be willing to stake your own signature on the solution as presented? If not, it is
not complete — finish it or say plainly which piece is missing and why.

## 7. Worked example — a process-change memo

An ops manager pitches a triage change to a COO. All figures are illustrative inputs,
not claimed statistics.

```
TO: COO                        FROM: Intake Ops Manager        DATE: <date>
RE: Decision — consolidate intake triage into one routed queue

ASK: APPROVE — option B by the 15th, so the transition lands before quarter close.

BOTTOM LINE: I recommend consolidating our three-team intake triage into a single
routed queue (option B): it removes the duplicate triage that is driving our SLA
misses, at a one-time setup cost of roughly 40 staff-hours. I need your approval
by the 15th.

BACKGROUND: Intake requests arrive in a shared mailbox and three teams triage
manually (Situation). Volume has roughly doubled since the merger and two teams
now routinely triage the same request while others wait — SLA misses are rising
month over month (Complication). How do we hold the SLA without adding headcount
(Question)? Consolidate triage into one routed queue (Answer).

OPTIONS:
  A. Do nothing — SLA misses keep growing with volume; duplicate triage continues
     to burn roughly a day of staff time per week (our log-based estimate).
  B. Single routed queue — ~40 staff-hours setup + a two-week transition;
     removes duplication at the source.                        ← RECOMMENDED
  C. Add a triage FTE — ongoing cost; absorbs volume but leaves the duplication
     mechanism in place.

RECOMMENDATION: B — it is the only option that removes the cause rather than
paying for the symptom, and the setup cost is one-time: 40 hours against the ~8
hours a week option A burns is five weeks of savings to repay it, starting after
the two-week transition — about seven weeks from approval, then it is free.

IF NOTHING IS DECIDED BY THE 15TH: the transition slips past quarter close and
we carry the SLA misses through the quarter-end reporting cycle.

SUPPORT (each reason first, its evidence attached):
(1) Duplication, not volume alone, is driving the misses — 31% of requests were
    triaged twice last month [request-log extract, attached].
(2) The routing rules already exist and are agreed — draft reviewed and signed off
    by all three team leads [draft, attached].
(3) The change is reversible — two-week transition with a named rollback point at
    day 5 [transition plan, attached].
```

Why this passes the tests: the ask is named twice with a date; every option is costed
including do-nothing; the recommendation gives its reason; silence has a stated price;
the COO could write "approved" on it and execution starts from the attachments. Note
what each SUPPORT line is — an *assertion* with its evidence hung underneath, not a list
of attachments; a bare attachment list is a bibliography, and a bibliography is not a
pyramid. The figures came from the request log, and the payback arithmetic is shown
rather than asserted — a memo's numbers get sourced and checked like any other claim.

## 8. Re-leading — IRAC vs BLUF, and the brief-up

**The attorney's version.** Law-school IRAC (Issue → Rule → Application → Conclusion)
trains conclusion-last — sound for exams, wrong for a client who needs to act. Re-lead
the same memo:

- *IRAC-led*: "Issue: whether the non-compete is enforceable against Ms. R. … Rule: …
  Application: … Conclusion (page 4): likely unenforceable; we recommend declining to
  pursue."
- *BLUF-led*: "Bottom line: the non-compete is likely unenforceable against Ms. R., and
  we recommend declining to pursue. Approval requested by Friday to send the
  declination letter. Three grounds follow, strongest first, with the risk analysis if a
  court disagrees."

Nothing in the analysis changes — the pyramid holds the R and the A — only the reader's
first thirty seconds change. The same re-lead fixes the analyst's methods-first report
and the developer's design doc that tours the architecture before naming the choice.

**The brief-up.** Compressing a 20-page artifact to one page: write the one-sentence
answer of each section, arrange those sentences into the pyramid, then keep only the
groups the decision needs. Map each memo line to the section it summarizes, so a
descending reader lands in the right place. What resists compression to one sentence
usually was not a finding.

## 9. Register and routing

- **Register** — short active sentences, point-first paragraphs, bold and bullets for
  scanning — is owned by `writing-skills:adams-smart-brevity`; run its pass on every
  memo. This file owns what the document argues and in what order; that skill owns the
  sentences.
- **Slides** → `data-analytics-bi-skills:assertion-evidence-deck` (one full-sentence
  claim per slide, proven by a visual — the pyramid's reasons become the slide
  headlines).
- **A meeting** → `collaboration-skills:meeting-design`; this memo is a natural
  pre-read for a silent start, and the decision rule in the room lives there.
- **Minutes-scale urgency, spoken** →
  `safety-and-reliability-skills:sbar-structured-communication`; a decision memo is a
  considered document, not an escalation channel.
- **Teaching, not deciding** → `writing-skills:explanation-design`.
