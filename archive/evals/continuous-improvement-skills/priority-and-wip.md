# Evals — continuous-improvement-skills:priority-and-wip

## 1. Positive trigger (should load the skill)
> "I'm drowning in tasks — I count fourteen things in flight, everything feels urgent, nothing
> is finishing, and new asks keep landing every day. Set me up with a personal kanban and a WIP
> limit, and tell me what to do when someone hands me a fifteenth thing."

Expected: skill loads; sweeps all work into one visible board (to-do / doing / done, nothing
left in inboxes or memory); sets a hard WIP limit of 2–3 on "doing" with the explicit
violation protocol (finish, move back, or drop — never silently expand); triages incoming work
with the urgent/important split while naming its failure mode (everything migrates to
urgent-important; the WIP limit, not better sorting, is the fix); timeboxes deep work and
batches shallow work; supplies a renegotiation script for the fifteenth thing rather than a
silent yes; schedules a weekly review to prune and re-justify; teaches the Little's Law
intuition for why starting more makes everything finish later.

## 2. Near-miss (should NOT load this skill)
> "Our month-end close takes nine days and the whole calendar seems to wait on one senior
> accountant's reconciliations. Where's the bottleneck, and how do we re-sequence the team
> around it?"

Expected: this is a system constraint in a multi-person process —
`continuous-improvement-skills:theory-of-constraints` owns it (five focusing steps,
drum-buffer-rope, subordinating the calendar). Priority-and-wip is that method scaled to one
person's attention, not a team's process. If priority-and-wip loads here, its description has
grown past the personal scope — tighten the "one person" framing and the Not-for line.

## 2b. Near-miss (adjacent-decision guard)
> "We're six months and a lot of budget into this system implementation and the vendor keeps
> slipping. We've come too far to stop now, right? Help me think about whether to keep going."

Expected: a continue-vs-revise decision under sunk-cost pressure —
`decision-science-skills:the-challenger` owns it (forward-looking costs only, burden of proof
on continuation, widened options). Priority-and-wip only flags that standing commitments get
re-justified in the weekly review and routes this very question outward. If it loads as the
primary skill here, the boundary line needs sharpening.

## 3. Quality rubric
A good response:
- **Does the task:** produces a working system, not advice — a concrete board with an initial
  sweep of every hiding place, a numeric WIP limit with the three-move violation protocol, a
  triage flow for arrivals, calendar-level timebox and batch placements, at least one
  renegotiation script the user could send verbatim, and a scheduled weekly review with a
  pruning step.
- **Teaches:** states Little's Law correctly (average WIP = throughput × average cycle time)
  and works the intuition — at fixed throughput, cycle time scales with WIP, so starting more
  makes everything finish later; explains why a visible board beats a memory-resident list
  (the mind is a bad kanban); why WIP limits feel slower and are faster; frames the whole
  method as theory of constraints for one person whose constraint is attention.
- **Stays honest:** holds the Eisenhower grid to its failure mode (quadrant inflation under
  pressure) instead of selling it as the answer; keeps context-switching costs qualitative —
  no invented percentages; presents Little's Law as directional intuition for personal work,
  not a scheduling calculator; treats an over-capacity week as something to renegotiate
  explicitly, never to absorb silently.
