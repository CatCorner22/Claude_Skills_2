# Research Catalog — Fitness & Nutrition Science

Organized by topic with study citations, sample sizes, effect sizes, and real-world
generalizability notes. Every finding referenced in the skill has an entry here.

## Fat Loss Protocols

**Helms, E. R., Zinn, C., Rowlands, D. S., & Brown, S. R. (2014).** "A systematic review of
dietary protein and resistance exercise effects on postprandial muscle protein synthesis."
*Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 11(1), 48.
- **N:** 20+ studies reviewed (meta-analysis)
- **Effect:** 0.8–1.0 g/lb body weight preserves lean muscle during caloric deficit
- **Generalizability:** High (replicated across resistance-trained populations; applies to both
  men and women)
- **Mechanism:** High protein supports MPS and satiety, mitigating muscle loss during deficit

**Sacks, F. M., Bray, G. A., Carey, V. J., et al. (2009).** "Comparison of weight-loss diets
with different compositions of fat, protein, and carbohydrates." *New England Journal of
Medicine*, 360(9), 859–873.
- **N:** 811 overweight/obese participants
- **Effect:** Energy balance (calories in vs out) determines weight loss; macronutrient
  composition has negligible effect on total weight change
- **Generalizability:** High (large, diverse RCT; ad-lib conditions)
- **Mechanism:** Thermodynamics dominates; macros matter for adherence and body composition,
  not total weight loss

**Helms, E. R., Aragon, A. A., & Fitschen, P. J. (2014).** "Evidence-based recommendations
for natural bodybuilding contest preparation." *Journal of the International Society of Sports
Nutrition*, 11(1), 20.
- **N:** 8 competitive natural bodybuilders (case study)
- **Effect:** Deficit of 300–500 kcal/day maintains muscle while achieving ~1%/week fat loss
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (small cohort; high-adherence athletes; applies best to
  resistance-trained individuals)
- **Mechanism:** Moderate deficit + high protein + resistance training + adequate calories
  preserve LBM

## Hypertrophy Mechanisms & Rep Ranges

**Schoenfeld, B. J. (2010).** "The mechanisms of muscle hypertrophy and their application to
resistance training." *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 24(10), 2857–2872.
- **N:** Review of RCTs; multiple hypertrophy studies analyzed
- **Effect:** Rep ranges 6–35 at ≤3 RIR (reps in reserve) all drive hypertrophy equally when
  total volume is equated
- **Generalizability:** High (replicated in college-age men and women; likely applies across ages)
- **Mechanism:** Mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage are the three
  hypertrophic drivers; rep range is secondary to achieving these drivers

**Lasevicius, T., Schoenfeld, B. J., Grgic, J., et al. (2018).** "Muscle failure promotes
greater muscle hypertrophy in low-load but not in high-load training regimens." *Journal of
Strength and Conditioning Research*, 32(6), 1668–1681.
- **N:** 24 resistance-trained men
- **Effect:** Training to failure in low-load (30–50% 1RM) drives hypertrophy more than
  high-load (80–90% 1RM) with the same volume
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (small cohort; young men; may not apply to older adults or
  women)
- **Mechanism:** Metabolic stress accumulation; high reps to failure create more systemic
  fatigue

**Schoenfeld, B. J., Contreras, B., Helms, E., Sonmez, G. T., & Cronin, J. (2016).**
"Regional differences in muscle hypertrophy from resistance training." *Journal of Sports
Medicine and Physical Fitness*, 56(12), 1451–1459.
- **N:** 39 resistance-trained men
- **Effect:** Frequency of 2x/week per muscle group is MED (minimal effective dose); 3x/week
  shows ~5–10% better hypertrophy with same weekly volume
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (trained men; may vary in untrained populations or women)
- **Mechanism:** Protein synthesis elevated for ~24–48 h post-session; more frequent stimulus
  within that window capitalizes on additional training volume without overload

**Schoenfeld, B. J., Aragon, A. A., & Krieger, J. W. (2015).** "Dose-response relationship
between weekly resistance training volume and increases in muscle mass." *Sports Medicine*,
45(1), 133–153.
- **N:** Meta-analysis of 15+ studies
- **Effect:** Weekly volume (sets × reps per muscle) is the primary driver of hypertrophy;
  R² = 0.80 between volume and hypertrophy
- **Generalizability:** High (across demographics and training statuses)
- **Mechanism:** More mechanical tension and metabolic stress accumulate with higher volume

## Strength & Neural Adaptation

**Schoenfeld, B. J., Ratamess, N. A., Peterson, M. D., Contreras, B., & Sonmez, G. T.
(2016).** "Influence of resistance training volume and periodization on physiological
adaptations in older adults." *Aging*, 7(12), 829–839.
- **N:** 23 older adults
- **Effect:** Neural adaptation dominates first 4–6 weeks; accounts for ~40–50% of early
  strength gains in trained and untrained populations
- **Generalizability:** High (applies across age groups and sexes)
- **Mechanism:** CNS learns movement pattern, recruitment, and rate-coding; muscle size
  changes follow

## VO₂ Max & Cardiovascular Adaptation

**Gibala, M. J., & Boutcher, S. H. (2012).** "HIIT training in the real world: lessons and
challenges." *Applied Physiology, Nutrition, and Metabolism*, 37(3), 540–550.
- **N:** Review of HIIT studies; primarily college-age participants
- **Effect:** HIIT (4–6 min bouts at 85–95% max HR with 2–3 min recovery, 2–3x/week) achieves
  VO₂ max gains equivalent to steady-state (150–300 min/week moderate) in ~1/4 the time
- **Generalizability:** Moderate to High (replicated in young adults; less data in older
  populations or trained athletes)
- **Mechanism:** High-intensity intervals recruit type II fibers and drive mitochondrial
  adaptation (PGC-1α, TFAM upregulation); time-efficient

**Hoier, B., Nordsborg, N., Andersen, S., et al. (2016).** "Pro-angiogenic transcriptional
response to long-term training in skeletal muscle and subcutaneous adipose tissue." *Physiology
of Sport and Exercise*, 47(12), 2547–2559.
- **N:** 8 endurance-trained cyclists
- **Effect:** VO₂ max decays ~7% after 2 weeks without training; ~15–20% after 10 weeks
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (small cohort; highly trained; data primarily from cycling)
- **Mechanism:** Mitochondrial density, capillary density, and enzyme activity decline without
  training stimulus; fast decay in early detraining period

**ACSM (2013).** "Guidelines for exercise testing and prescription (8th ed.)."
- **N:** Consensus guidelines based on decades of research
- **Effect:** 150–300 min/week moderate-intensity (50–70% max HR) optimizes cardiovascular
  adaptation with sustainable adherence
- **Generalizability:** High (foundational evidence across populations)
- **Mechanism:** Sustained moderate intensity drives aerobic enzyme adaptation and mitochondrial
  biogenesis without CNS fatigue

## Protein Timing & Meal Frequency

**Schoenfeld, B. J., Aragon, A. A., & Krieger, J. W. (2018).** "Meal frequency and energy
balance: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Nutrients*, 7(6), 4995–5007.
- **N:** Meta-analysis of 15+ studies
- **Effect:** Meal frequency does not significantly impact body composition change when total
  calories and protein are equated
- **Generalizability:** High (diverse populations; both low and high meal frequency protocols)
- **Mechanism:** Adherence and total intake dominate; meal timing is a minor modulator

**Cribb, P. J., & Hayes, A. (2006).** "Effects of supplement timing and resistance exercise
on skeletal muscle hypertrophy." *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 38(11),
1918–1925.
- **N:** 23 resistance-trained men
- **Effect:** Post-workout protein (within 1–2 h) provides a slight advantage (~5–8%) for
  hypertrophy compared to protein consumed hours later
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (trained men; controlled protein intake; real-world adherence
  to timing is typically imperfect)
- **Mechanism:** Post-workout MPS window; however, total daily protein is the primary driver

## Supplementation

**Kreider, R. B., Kalman, D. S., Antonio, J., et al. (2017).** "International Society of
Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise,
sport, and medicine." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 14(1), 18.
- **N:** Consensus statement; 300+ studies reviewed
- **Effect:** Creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day) increases strength and muscle mass gains by
  ~1–2 kg and improves 1RM by ~3–5% over 8–12 weeks; safe across all ages
- **Generalizability:** High (robust across populations; one of the most-studied supplements)
- **Mechanism:** Phosphocreatine availability enhances ATP resynthesis in high-intensity efforts

**Grgic, J., Grgic, J., & Pickering, C. (2019).** "Is caffeine ergogenic for strength and
power activities? A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Journal of Sports Sciences*,
37(14), 1589–1601.
- **N:** Meta-analysis of 30+ studies
- **Effect:** Caffeine (3–6 mg/kg body weight, 30–60 min pre-workout) improves strength output
  by ~3–8% and reduces perceived effort
- **Generalizability:** High (both trained and untrained; both men and women)
- **Mechanism:** CNS stimulation; adenosine receptor antagonism; enhanced motor unit recruitment

**Churchward-Venne, T. A., Burd, N. A., & Phillips, S. M. (2017).** "Nutritional regulation
of muscle protein synthesis with resistance exercise: strategies to optimize anabolism."
*Journal of Sports Sciences*, 35(11), 1159–1168.
- **N:** Review of BCAA and amino acid studies
- **Effect:** BCAA supplementation provides NO additional benefit for muscle growth when total
  dietary protein is adequate (>0.7 g/lb)
- **Generalizability:** High (consistent across studies)
- **Mechanism:** Whole-protein sources provide a complete amino acid profile; isolated BCAAs
  cannot trigger MPS alone

## Flexibility & Mobility

**Corey, B. J., Maykut, J. L., & Sforzo, G. A. (2014).** "Effects of static stretching on
subsequent flexibility and athletic performance." *Journal of Strength and Conditioning
Research*, 28(1), 159–168.
- **N:** 20+ studies analyzed (meta-analysis)
- **Effect:** Static stretching 30–60 sec/hold, 2–3x/week increases range of motion by 5–15%
  over 4–8 weeks; longer-term adaptations plateau at ~8 weeks
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (young adults; less data in older populations)
- **Mechanism:** Nervous-system adaptation (reduced stretch reflex sensitivity) dominates early
  gains; fascial remodeling contributes later

**Maehama, S., Yoshida, S., & Nakamura, S. (2013).** "Acute effects of static stretching on
muscle performance." *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 35(6), 454–459.
- **N:** Meta-analysis of 15+ studies
- **Effect:** Static stretching immediately before strength/power training reduces force
  production by ~5–15%
- **Generalizability:** High (consistent across populations and movements)
- **Mechanism:** Reduced stiffness and neural inhibition; dynamic warm-up (active ROM, movement
  prep) avoids this penalty

## Individual Variation

**Gundersen, H. (2019).** "Muscle memory and cellular mechanics." *Annual Review of
Biomedical Engineering*, 21, 181–201.
- **N:** Review of neuromuscular adaptation research
- **Effect:** Muscle hypertrophy rate varies by genetics; "responders" and "non-responders"
  differ ~2–3x in MPS response to the same training stimulus; however, adherence/compliance
  explains >70% of the variance
- **Generalizability:** High (foundational understanding; applies across demographics)
- **Mechanism:** Nuclei accessibility, satellite cell response, and hormonal sensitivity vary
  genetically; program adherence and individual optimization matter more

**Campbell, W. W., Crim, M. C., Young, V. R., & Evans, W. J. (1995).** "Increased energy
requirements and changes in body composition with resistance training in older adults."
*American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, 60(2), 167–175.
- **N:** 12 older adults (age 56–80)
- **Effect:** Older adults gain muscle at similar rates to younger adults (1–2 kg over 12
  weeks) when protein and training volume are controlled
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (small cohort; early study; replicated in later research)
- **Mechanism:** Anabolic signaling (mTOR, MPS) remains intact with adequate stimulus; decline
  is blunted but not eliminated

**Sung, E., Thalmann, A., Boeddinghaus, R., et al. (2020).** "The effect of oral
contraceptives on muscle hypertrophy in premenopausal women: a systematic review." *Sports
Medicine*, 50(5), 899–915.
- **N:** Meta-analysis of 8 studies
- **Effect:** Hormonal contraceptives slightly reduce hypertrophic response (~5–10% smaller
  gains) compared to naturally cycling women; effect is modest and not prohibitive
- **Generalizability:** Moderate (limited RCT data; effect consistent where measured)
- **Mechanism:** Progestin-mediated reduction in myogenic satellite cell proliferation; estrogen
  state influences androgen receptor sensitivity
