## §1 The Laws of Combat on projects

**Cover and Move** — teams exist to support each other, not to defend lanes.
- Worked example: front office schedules a hygiene patient into a slot clinical can't
  turn over in time. Lane thinking: "scheduling's error." Cover and Move: clinical
  publishes turnover times to scheduling; scheduling flags tight slots at booking; each
  team owns making the OTHER team's job work.
- Project form: for every pair of dependent teams, write two sentences — "X gives Y ___
  by ___" and "if X slips, Y loses ___." Review the pairs, not the org chart.

**Simple** — the plan must survive the read-back of its most junior executor.
- Test: have the newest team member (or the model roleplaying them) repeat the plan
  back. Every stumble marks a cut or a clarification. A plan that needs its author
  present is not a plan; it's a performance.
- Worked example: a 14-step close-week email becomes "Three things this week: cash by
  Tuesday, recons by Thursday, review Friday 9. If anything blocks you, say so the hour
  it happens."

**Prioritize and Execute** — "Relax. Look around. Make a call."
1. Detach (step back from the screen; literally stand up).
2. Name every open fire in one line each.
3. Pick the single highest priority — the one that, unresolved, makes the others worse.
4. Execute on it with the whole team; ignore the rest until re-assessment.
5. Re-assess and repeat. The failure mode is triaging from inside one fire.

**Decentralized Command** — intent plus bounds beats instructions.
- Write intent (purpose / key tasks / end state — the format is in
  `decision-science-skills:tabletop-wargaming`, its `references/commanders-intent.md`).
- Per delegate: what they decide alone; what they decide and inform; what they must
  flag first. Review the bounds when they're never used (too tight) or always exceeded
  (too loose).

## §2 Ownership-rewrite patterns

| Blame form | Ownership form |
|---|---|
| "The vendor missed the cutoff" | "I didn't verify the vendor's cutoff; I've set a 6 a.m. delivery check and a fallback export" |
| "The other team never told us" | "I didn't build a channel where they'd tell us; standing sync starts this week" |
| "Leadership keeps changing priorities" | "I haven't shown leadership the cost of the churn; the switch-cost table goes in Friday's brief" |
| "The users keep doing it wrong" | "The design lets them do it wrong; the form now blocks the error" (poka-yoke) |
| "We ran out of time" | "I planned no buffer and escalated late; next cycle has both, and here's the trigger" |

Rules: subject of every sentence is someone in the room; every rewrite ends in an
action with a date; systemic causes keep their system fix — the rewrite adds the owner
of that fix, it does not replace it with guilt.

## §3 Dichotomy of Leadership check table

Every leadership virtue fails at its extreme. Audit which side you're on:

| Balanced | Failing left | Failing right |
|---|---|---|
| Involved leadership | Micromanaging every task | Abdicating ("they'll figure it out") |
| Aggressive by default | Reckless (no risk assessment) | Passive (waiting for certainty) |
| Disciplined | Rigid (process over mission) | Loose (nothing repeatable) |
| Confident | Arrogant (stops listening) | Insecure (decisions wobble) |
| Holds the standard | Nitpicks everything | Tolerates what it preaches against |
| Trains hard | Burns the team down | Never rehearses (first rep is live) |

The check: for the current situation, name the row in play and the side being failed —
then move one notch toward center, not to the opposite extreme.

## §4 Leading up and down the chain

- **Down**: the team's failure is your briefing failure until proven otherwise. If they
  did the wrong thing, the intent wasn't clear; fix the brief before judging the team.
- **Up**: the boss's "bad call" is your communication failure until you've delivered the
  full picture in their language — SBAR format
  (`safety-and-reliability-skills:sbar-structured-communication`), a recommendation, and
  the cost of alternatives. Ask for what the mission needs; don't resent silently.
- "It's not what you preach, it's what you tolerate": the standard is whatever you last
  let slide — down the chain AND up it.

## §5 Voice card

Calm. Direct. Short sentences. Setback → "Good." → the lesson it just paid for and the
next action. Intensity aims at the problem; respect aims at the people. No profanity,
no humiliation, no drill-sergeant cosplay at teammates. Discipline equals freedom:
routines and standards are presented as what buys the team slack, never as punishment.
