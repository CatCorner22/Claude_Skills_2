# Feedback that lands (giving and receiving)

## When to use
- Preparing to tell someone their behavior is causing a problem — and wanting both the
  change and the relationship intact afterward.
- Rewriting a performance note or a code-review comment whose tone reads as an attack
  on the person rather than a description of the behavior.
- The conversation already went sideways ("they got defensive") — diagnosing what
  triggered and planning the re-approach.
- On the receiving end: extracting the usable data from feedback, however clumsily it
  was delivered, and managing your own reaction while you do.
- Making praise as useful as criticism — specific, evidence-backed, naming the
  behavior (the same praise rule `coding-agent-skills:the-foreman` applies to
  inspection findings).
- A feedback conversation that must end in a concrete agreement borrows the
  owner-and-date read-back from sibling `collaboration-skills:meeting-design`.
- Not for: critique of a submitted work product → see
  `coding-agent-skills:sparring-partner`; that skill evaluates the work, this one
  addresses a person's behavior.
- Not for: code-review mechanics (branching, PR flow, reading a diff) → see
  `coding-agent-skills:git-and-code-review`; this skill upgrades the comment's tone,
  that one the process around it.
- Not for: team-failure debriefs → see `decision-science-skills:after-action-review`
  for the blameless four-question debrief, and `coding-agent-skills:extreme-ownership`
  for the ownership doctrine around it.

## Do it
Worked before/after rewrites, the COIN variant, the feedforward pattern, and the
receiving field guide are in `references/feedback-method.md`.

**Giving**
1. **Pick one topic.** One behavior per conversation. A list of grievances is an
   indictment; a single specific behavior is a conversation.
2. **Draft as SBI** — the Center for Creative Leadership's Situation-Behavior-Impact
   model:
   - *Situation*: one specific time and place ("in Tuesday's planning call"), never
     "always" or "lately".
   - *Behavior*: what a camera would have recorded ("you answered before she finished
     her sentence, three times") — never the inferred character or motive ("you're
     dismissive", "you don't respect her").
   - *Impact*: your own experience, owned as yours ("I lost her input on the two
     items I most needed it for; I noticed she stopped offering").
3. **Frame the request forward** — Marshall Goldsmith's feedforward: "next time,
   would you let her finish and then respond?" beats relitigating Tuesday. The past
   is evidence; the request lives in the future, where change is actually possible.
4. **Calibrate privacy and timing.** Criticism lands in private, close to the event,
   and never mid-crisis or mid-audience; praise can be public if the receiver wants
   that. Feedback hoarded for the formal performance cycle arrives months too late
   to use.
5. **Give praise the same machinery.** Situation, behavior, impact, evidence — "the
   migration runbook was good" teaches nothing; naming exactly what made it good
   makes the behavior repeatable.
6. **Rehearse before delivering.** The assistant plays the receiver — including a
   defensive one — so the first live run is not the real conversation.

**Receiving**
7. **Name which trigger is firing** before you respond to anything — Stone & Heen's
   three (from *Thanks for the Feedback*): a *truth* trigger ("that's just wrong"), a
   *relationship* trigger ("not from *you*"), an *identity* trigger ("then what am
   I?"). Naming it to yourself buys back the ability to hear the content. "Give me a
   second with that" is a complete sentence while you do it.
8. **Separate the data from the delivery.** Badly delivered truth is still truth;
   discard the packaging, keep the information. This step needs step 7 first — a
   triggered listener cannot tell the two apart.
9. **Ask for the behavior behind the label.** "You're told you're 'not strategic' —
   what did I do or not do, and when?" converts a verdict into data you can act on.
10. **Close the loop.** Say what you will try, then report back — it turns one
    awkward conversation into a channel that stays open.

Division of labor: the assistant drafts the SBI script from a messy vent (stripping
character-language as it goes), plays the receiver in rehearsal, and audits written
comments for character-language. The human judges the relationship, the moment, and
whether to have the conversation at all — and then has it.

## Why / learn
Behavior-language lands where character-language triggers defense because of the
fundamental attribution error, social psychology's name for a stubborn asymmetry: we
explain other people's actions by their character ("late because he's careless") and
our own by our circumstances ("late because of the pileup"). Feedback aimed at
character therefore strikes the receiver as *factually wrong from the inside* — they
can see their circumstances, and you are ignoring them — so they argue the verdict
instead of hearing the observation. A described behavior sidesteps the whole fight:
"you answered before she finished, three times" is checkable, specific, and shares
the interpretation problem with the receiver instead of pre-convicting them.

Impact stated as your own experience is undeniable in a way accusation never is.
"You're careless" invites a rebuttal; "I spent two hours re-checking the figures
because the tie-out was missing" reports an event in your world that the receiver
cannot argue away — and it explains *why the behavior matters*, which is the part
that motivates change. The moment impact drifts into accusation ("you made everyone
feel ignored"), it becomes debatable again: you are now claiming to know other
people's inner states, and the conversation moves there instead of forward.

Feedforward works because the past is the only thing neither of you can change.
Relitigating Tuesday makes the receiver a defendant; "next time, would you…" makes
them a collaborator on a future both of you can still shape. And feedback given close
to the event, one topic at a time, is usable feedback; hoarding it for the formal
review converts help into judgment — by the time it arrives, the examples are stale,
the pattern is entrenched, and the setting has raised the stakes so high that
listening is hardest exactly when the material is heaviest.

Receiving deserves equal weight because the receiver, not the giver, controls whether
feedback becomes change. Stone & Heen's insight is that the three triggers are not
character flaws; they are predictable reactions that *block the data channel* — and a
named reaction loosens its grip. The skill of asking for the behavior behind a vague
label is the receiving-side mirror of SBI: when the giver hands you a verdict, you
reconstruct the observation yourself.

## Common mistakes
- "You always…" / "you never…" → one specific situation; totalizing words convert an
  observation into a character trial.
- Inferred motive ("you don't care about quality") → the camera test: describe what
  was observable, share the interpretation question instead of answering it for them.
- The compliment sandwich → one topic, plainly; burying criticism teaches people to
  distrust your praise.
- Vague praise ("great job on the release") → same specificity as criticism: what
  behavior, what impact, so it can be repeated on purpose.
- Stacking six months of examples into one conversation → the list proves a verdict,
  not a behavior; pick the most recent clean example and let the rest go.
- Relitigating the incident until they concede → state it once, then move to the
  forward request; concession is not the goal, change is.
- Public criticism because the moment was convenient → privacy is part of the
  message; an audience turns feedback into a status event.
- Responding to feedback while a trigger is firing → name the trigger first (truth /
  relationship / identity); reply to the content after it loosens.
- Dismissing feedback because the delivery was clumsy → separate data from delivery;
  the messenger's skill is not evidence about the message.

## Tailor to your environment
Record house norms in `references/your-environment.md`: where written feedback lives
(review tools, performance systems), your team's norms on public praise, the formal
performance-cycle rhythm this skill front-runs, and any house language for review
comments. Real names, past incidents, and anything about specific colleagues belong
in `your-environment.private.md` (git-ignored), never in a committed file.

**Keep your filled-in copy outside the plugin.** This file ships as a *template* and lives inside
the installed plugin, where a `/plugin marketplace update` can overwrite it or refuse to run against
a dirty tree. Copy it into your own project — `.claude/skills-env/feedback-that-lands.md` works well — fill it in
there, and point this skill at that copy. Your specifics then survive updates and stay somewhere you
own rather than in a cache you may not realise is disposable.
