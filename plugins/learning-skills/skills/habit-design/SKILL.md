# Habit design (make it automatic)

A behavior repeated by decision is paid for every single time; a behavior repeated by habit
is paid for once, at design time. This skill does the design: cue, routine, reward, written
as an implementation intention — Gollwitzer's if-then form, rendered here as "when X happens,
I will Y," one of the better-replicated effects in behavior-change research — stacked onto
something stable, sized below the failure threshold, and given a scheduled renegotiation so
it never becomes clutter.

## When to use
- Making one repeated behavior automatic: the daily position check before anything else, the
  rep log after each drill session, the inbox-zero pass, the evening shutdown routine.
- A recurring failure of the form "I keep forgetting to X" or "I only do X when I have the
  energy."
- Stopping a repeated behavior — which is designed here as *replacing* its routine, since
  cues cannot be deleted, only re-pointed.
- Repairing a habit that keeps collapsing (usually a design defect: too big, vague cue,
  reward too distant, environment fighting it).
- Not for: drive and effort on a hard, chosen goal → see
  `coding-agent-skills:stay-hard-accountability` — the division of labor: habit design
  automates the routine precisely so that drive is spent on the hard things only drive can do.
- Not for: standardizing a team's process → see `continuous-improvement-skills:standard-work`.
  The duality is worth naming: a habit is personal standard work — same "current best-known
  method, stabilized," different unit. When the behavior belongs to more than one person,
  write the SOP instead.
- Making study sessions happen on schedule pairs naturally with
  `learning-skills:spaced-retrieval-learning`: that skill runs the session's content, this
  one makes attendance automatic.

## Do it
The design worksheet, implementation-intention patterns, the friction audit, the
renegotiation protocol, and the failure-mode table are in `references/habit-method.md`.

1. **Pick one habit.** One. Parallel habit launches compete for the same design attention
   and the same disrupted contexts; serial wins. Ask what the user wants to be true in six
   months, then pick the single behavior with the best leverage on it.
2. **Shrink it below the failure threshold.** Define the smallest version that still counts
   — small enough that doing it is easier than justifying the skip: one flashcard session of
   five minutes, one line in the rep log, opening the position worksheet. The full-sized
   ambition is recorded as the *later* scale target, not the starting requirement.
3. **Choose a cue that already happens reliably.** Best: stack onto an existing stable habit
   ("after I pour the first coffee…"). Also workable: a fixed event or place. Not workable:
   a time of day the user doesn't already notice, or "in the morning" (too vague to fire).
4. **Write the implementation intention verbatim.** "When <cue>, I will <tiny routine> in
   <place>." Gollwitzer & Sheeran's 2006 meta-analysis found that pre-deciding the
   when-where-how of acting substantially raises the rate of actually doing it: d ≈ 0.65,
   medium-to-large, across 94 independent tests — a strong return on one written sentence.
   Have the user say it back; write it into the worksheet. Add a coping form for the known
   obstacle: "if <obstacle>, then <planned response>."
5. **Engineer the friction.** Make the good path one step shorter (stage it the night
   before: worksheet already open, shoes by the door) and the competing path one step longer
   (app logged out, snack not in the house). The friction audit in the reference walks the
   steps of each path. Environment changes are the cheapest, most reliable lever — apply
   them before asking anything of willpower.
6. **Attach an immediate reward or closure signal.** The loop trains on what follows the
   routine *now* — a checked box, the satisfaction of a visible tally, the first sip of the
   good coffee reserved for after. Distant rewards (health, promotion) motivate the design
   but cannot train the loop.
7. **Track presence, not streaks.** A simple did/didn't record. A missed day is data — what
   happened to the cue? did friction creep back? — and gets one diagnostic question, zero
   guilt. Two consecutive misses is a design signal: shrink the routine or move the cue.
   Never "start over from day zero"; there is no day zero, only the record.
8. **Scale only after stability, and schedule the renegotiation.** After weeks of reliable
   execution at the tiny size, grow the routine one notch. At design time, set a date (e.g.,
   six or eight weeks out) to deliberately keep, resize, or retire the habit — retirement is
   a legitimate outcome, not a failure.

## Why / learn
**Why willpower is the wrong tool for repeated behavior.** Every fresh decision to act draws
on a limited budget of attention and self-command, and repeated behaviors present the
decision daily — including on the depleted days, which is exactly when decisions go wrong.
Automaticity removes the decision: the cue fires the routine the way the doorway fires
reaching for the light switch. The goal of habit design is not stronger resolve; it is a
behavior that no longer consults resolve at all. Spend design effort once so willpower can be
spent where only willpower works — which is why the hard-goal push belongs to
`coding-agent-skills:stay-hard-accountability` and the repetition belongs here.

**Habits live in environments.** The cue-routine link is learned in a context — a place, a
preceding action, a time-of-day texture — and fires when the context recurs. This is why
habits evaporate on travel and after moves or job changes, and why the highest-leverage move
is changing the environment rather than the self: re-point the cue, re-stage the room, and
the behavior follows. It also means a broken habit after a context change is a re-design
task, not a character verdict.

**Why implementation intentions work.** The sentence "when X, I will Y" delegates initiation
to the environment: the decision is made once, in advance, and the cue thereafter triggers
the act without in-the-moment deliberation — the fragile step where good intentions
historically die. That mechanism is why the wording is concrete and situational ("when I
close the 9:30 stand-up") rather than aspirational ("I'll do it daily").

**Why streak-guilt backfires.** A streak makes the record the point. One miss then destroys
the accumulated asset, and the rational-feeling response to a destroyed asset is abandonment
— the "what the hell" collapse after a single lapse. Presence tracking keeps the information
(rate, pattern of misses, cue failures) while removing the cliff. A missed day is one row of
data; treating it as a moral event converts a design signal into shame, and shame is not a
scheduling tool.

**Why tiny, and for how long.** The thing under training first is the cue→routine link, not
the outcome; consistency builds the link, and size can be added to a link that exists.
Automaticity develops over weeks to months and varies widely with the behavior's complexity —
there is no fixed "21 days," and any precise universal number is folklore.

**On sources.** The cue-routine-reward loop, habit stacking, tiny-first sizing, and
environment design as popularly packaged by Fogg (*Tiny Habits*) and Clear (*Atomic Habits*)
are practitioner syntheses — useful, field-tested operationalizations built atop the research
base (Gollwitzer's implementation intentions; the psychology of automaticity and
context-dependent behavior) rather than research findings themselves. This skill uses their
vocabulary where it is the clearest available and rests its claims on the research layer.

## Common mistakes
- Starting at the ambitious size ("45 minutes daily") → sized above the failure threshold;
  shrink until skipping is harder to justify than doing.
- Launching several habits at once → they compete and all lose; one at a time, serial.
- A vague cue ("in the mornings", "when I have time") → never fires; bind to a specific
  event that already happens.
- Counting on the outcome as the reward → too distant to train the loop; attach an immediate
  closure signal.
- Streak apps and day-zero resets → the cliff causes the collapse; track presence and treat
  misses as diagnostics.
- Fighting a hostile environment with resolve → move the friction first; the environment
  wins the rematch every time.
- Trying to delete a bad habit outright → the cue persists; design a replacement routine for
  the same cue.
- No end-of-life plan → zombie habits accumulate and discredit the system; renegotiation
  date at design time.
- Using habit machinery for a one-off hard push (a filing deadline, a brutal sprint) → wrong
  tool; see `coding-agent-skills:stay-hard-accountability`.

## Tailor to your environment
Record your standing setup in `references/your-environment.md`: your stable anchor habits
(the stack-onto points), the current habit under construction with its written implementation
intention, the presence tracker's location, and your renegotiation calendar. Keep committed
content structural — health specifics, personal routines you'd rather not publish, or
anything sensitive goes in `your-environment.private.md` (git-ignored), never in a committed
file.

**Keep your filled-in copy outside the plugin.** This file ships as a *template* and lives inside
the installed plugin, where a `/plugin marketplace update` can overwrite it or refuse to run against
a dirty tree. Copy it into your own project — `.claude/skills-env/habit-design.md` works well — fill it in
there, and point this skill at that copy. Your specifics then survive updates and stay somewhere you
own rather than in a cache you may not realise is disposable.
