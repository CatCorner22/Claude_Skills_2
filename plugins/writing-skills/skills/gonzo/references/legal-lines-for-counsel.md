# Legal lines — the doctrine map for counsel

You're the attorney; this is the Desk's map of where the rail is, offered craft-side —
your judgment governs, not this file. It exists because gonzo's founding wager (blur
the narrator, never the facts) happens to track the defamation doctrine remarkably
well, which is part of why the form survived its own excesses.

Contents: 1. Why gonzo mostly stays on the road · 2. The doctrine map · 3. The
practical rails · 4. The canon's own cautionary tales

## 1. Why gonzo mostly stays on the road
The form's signature moves — obvious exaggeration, impossible imagery, a narrator whose
reliability is visibly compromised — are exactly the features that keep heated language
from being read as factual assertion. The wilder the register, the clearer it is that
no reasonable reader takes it literally. The danger zone is the opposite of what
intuition says: not the fever passages, but the FLAT ones — the deadpan-procedural
register is where an invented "fact" could read as a fact. So the rule of craft and
the rule of caution are the same rule: keep the fiction in the narrator's head, keep
the record true.

## 2. The doctrine map (U.S. framework — verify currency and your jurisdiction)
- **Rhetorical hyperbole is not actionable**: *Greenbelt Cooperative Publishing Ass'n
  v. Bresler* (1970) — "blackmail" describing hard negotiation wasn't a crime
  accusation; *Old Dominion Branch No. 496, Letter Carriers v. Austin* (1974) —
  "traitor" in rhetorical context, loose figurative language. The reasonable-reader
  test does the work: language no reasonable reader takes as factual assertion.
- **But there is no wholesale "opinion" privilege**: *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*
  (1990) — "in my opinion" doesn't immunize a statement that implies a provably false
  fact ("in my opinion he perjured himself" still implies perjury). The question is
  always whether a provably false factual assertion hides inside the framing.
- **Parody of public figures**: *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell* (1988) — outrageous
  parody that no reasonable reader believes describes actual events doesn't support
  IIED/defamation for public figures. The ad at issue was labeled; labeling helps.
- **Public officials/figures**: *New York Times v. Sullivan* (1964) actual-malice
  standard; extended to public figures via *Curtis Publishing v. Butts* (1967).
  Private individuals get more protection (*Gertz v. Robert Welch* (1974)) — which is
  the doctrinal echo of the craft rule: aim at public conduct, not private people.
- **"Of and concerning"**: composites and pseudonyms don't protect if a real,
  identifiable person is recognizable and defamed. If a character wears one real
  person's haircut, job, and parking spot, the name change is decoration.

## 3. The practical rails (each is also better craft)
1. Load-bearing facts get verified before the dispatch ships — dates, quotes, who did
   what. The fever is scaffolding on a true frame.
2. Real quotes only real, attributed; invented dialogue unmistakably the narrator's
   fever (impossible circumstances, visibly hallucinatory frame).
3. No invented factual allegations about actual persons — not as a joke, not as a
   "rumor," not with a wink. (See §4.)
4. Savagery targets public conduct, institutions, ideas, and the powerful in their
   public capacity.
5. When a passage could be read two ways — figurative or factual — make it MORE
   figurative, not less savage. The fix is craft, not cowardice.
6. Venue awareness: what's cognizable-as-hyperbole in a labeled gonzo dispatch reads
   differently pasted into a demand letter. The register never goes on the record
   (route record-writing to `writing-skills:adams-smart-brevity`).

## 4. The canon's own cautionary tales
- **The ibogaine affair** (Campaign Trail '72): the author printed a "rumor" about a
  candidate's exotic pharmacology — a rumor he himself had started — and the flat,
  reportorial register carried it into circulation as quasi-fact; his later defense
  was that he'd only reported the rumor's existence. It is the perfect specimen: the
  damage came from the DEADPAN register, exactly as §1 predicts, and "I said there
  was a rumor" is the epitaph of the defense. This skill treats the episode as the
  line it will not cross: funny, famous, and not available. (Full sourcing in
  gonzo-canon.md.)
- **The attorney identification flap**: the Vegas book's fictionalized "300-pound
  Samoan attorney" was a thinly veiled real person (Oscar Zeta Acosta, who was neither
  Samoan nor anonymous and objected to the veil) — the "of and concerning" lesson from
  inside the canon itself: the disguise you're sure is a disguise may not be one.
  (Details and sourcing in gonzo-canon.md.)
