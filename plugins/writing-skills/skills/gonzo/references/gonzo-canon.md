## 1. Life anchors
- Born July 18, 1937, Louisville, Kentucky; died February 20, 2005, by his own hand at
  Owl Farm, Woody Creek, Colorado. [snippet-only, ×5]
- Air Force: sports editor of Eglin AFB's *Command Courier* (covering a base team
  stocked with future pros), moonlighting anonymously at a local paper against
  regulations; recommended for early honorable discharge by his commanding officer
  (date reported as both Nov 1957 and June 1958 — unsettled). The tale that he wrote a
  mock press release about his own discharge is thinly attested — treat as anecdote.
  [snippet-only, ×4]
- Puerto Rico, 1960: *El Sportivo*, a San Juan sports paper that folded in months;
  the seed of *The Rum Diary*. [snippet-only, ×3]
- **The discipline under the chaos — well attested:** as a young man (Time Inc. copy
  boy era) he retyped *The Great Gatsby* and *A Farewell to Arms* to feel the rhythm
  in his hands — his own account ("If you type out somebody's work, you learn a lot
  about it. Amazingly it's like music") plus independent tellings. [snippet-only, ×4]
- Hell's Angels: 1965 piece for *The Nation* → roughly a year riding with the club →
  the Labor Day 1966 "stomping" that ended it. [snippet-only, ×4]
- Freak Power, 1970: ran for Pitkin County sheriff — decriminalize drugs, disarm the
  deputies, sod the streets, punish "land rape," rename Aspen "Fat City." Lost by a
  few hundred votes (margin loosely reported: ~300 to ~500). [snippet-only, ×4]
- Rolling Stone from "The Battle of Aspen" (RS #67, Oct 1, 1970); the National Affairs
  Desk as his standing rubric [desk name itself weakly attested in this pass — ×1;
  standard lore, held as such]. The "mojo wire": his name for the Xerox Telecopier,
  an early ~7-minute-per-page fax he used to file at the last possible hour.
  [snippet-only, ×3]
- Late column: ESPN Page 2's "Hey Rube," 2000–2005. [snippet-only, ×3]
- Funeral, August 20, 2005: ashes fired from a cannon atop a 153-foot tower shaped
  like the two-thumbed gonzo fist, organized and funded by Johnny Depp. [×4] Cost
  figures ($2M–$5M) contested — quote none as fact.

## 2. The origin of gonzo
- "The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved," *Scanlan's Monthly*, June 1970 —
  first collaboration with Ralph Steadman. [snippet-only, ×4]
- The blown deadline: unable to compose, he tore numbered pages from his notebook and
  sent them raw. [×3] **Caveat:** the drama of the telling rests chiefly on Thompson's
  and Steadman's own retrospectives — the published piece's raw texture is real; the
  legend of its filing is self-mythologized. Hold it the way this library holds the
  space-pen story: instructive, and labeled.
- Bill Cardoso (*Boston Globe Sunday Magazine*) supplied the word: "This is it, this
  is pure Gonzo. If this is a start, keep rolling." [snippet-only, ×3]
- The word's etymology is honestly unresolved: Cardoso's own accounts (South Boston
  bar slang for "last man standing"; French-Canadian "gonzeaux") are spurious per
  wordorigins.org; OED proposes Italian *gonzo* (fool) / Spanish *ganso* (goose);
  a rival theory cites James Booker's 1960 instrumental "Gonzo." Present all as
  legend. [snippet-only, ×4]

## 3. The canon (what each work contributes to the voice)
- ***Hell's Angels*** (1966/67 — edition dating varies) — total immersion; the
  narrator as instrument. [×3]
- **"The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved" (1970)** — the pivot: the reporter's
  failure becomes the story; Steadman's grotesques fuse to the prose. [×4]
- **"Strange Rumblings in Aztlan" (RS, Apr 29, 1971)** — the Rubén Salazar killing
  (L.A. Times journalist, tear-gas projectile, sheriff's deputy, Chicano Moratorium,
  Aug 29, 1970); Oscar Zeta Acosta as guide; the drive-to-Vegas-to-talk-freely that
  seeded the next book. Gonzo's moral seriousness on display — the method exists
  because official accounts couldn't be trusted. [×4]
- ***Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas*** (RS Nov 1971 as Raoul Duke, two parts; book
  1972) — born of a ~250-word Sports Illustrated caption assignment that came back
  ~2,500 words and "aggressively rejected." The actual subject is the death of the
  American Dream; the **wave speech closes Part One, chapter 8** — the elegiac
  register's high-water mark, literally. [×4]
- ***Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72*** (1973) — the invective register at
  full power, and the fact-fiction problem at its sharpest (§7). Mankiewicz's verdict
  ("least factual, most accurate") lives here. [×3]
- ***The Curse of Lono*** (1983) — late gonzo straining for the old voltage; honest
  marker of the decline. [×3]
- ***Generation of Swine*** (1988) — the invective register industrialized into
  column-length units (100 SF Examiner columns). [×3]
- ***Better Than Sex*** (1994) — composed largely of his faxes; the mojo-wire method
  become the book. [×2]
- **The letters** — *The Proud Highway* (1997) and *Fear and Loathing in America*
  (2000), ed. Brinkley: the evidentiary backbone for dating his phrases, and the
  proof of the meticulous rewriter under the myth. [×2]
- ***The Rum Diary*** (begun 1959, published 1998 after Depp found the manuscript at
  Owl Farm). [×3]

## 4. Quotes — attested vs. misattributed
Quote briefly, attribute always. Attested:
- "So much for Objective Journalism... there is no such thing as Objective
  Journalism" (with the box-scores exception) — *Campaign Trail '72*. [×3; tag-line
  wording varies by edition]
- "Buy the ticket, take the ride." — *F&L in Las Vegas*. [×2; circulating versions
  often fuse invented framing text — check surroundings]
- "Too weird to live, and too rare to die" — *F&L in Las Vegas*, said OF the
  attorney character, not of Thompson, though it became his epitaph. [×3]
- "When the going gets weird, the weird turn pro." — earliest verifiable print:
  "Fear and Loathing at the Super Bowl," Rolling Stone, Feb 1974. Almost universally
  mis-cited to the Vegas book — it is not in that text. [×2]
- The phrase "fear and loathing" first appears in his Nov 22, 1963 letter (the day of
  the JFK assassination) to William Kennedy — eight years before Vegas (*The Proud
  Highway*). Scholars note earlier occurrences in Thomas Wolfe and the Kierkegaard
  resonance; Thompson believed it his own. [×3]
- The "cruel and shallow money trench" line is about **TV** (SF Examiner 1985 /
  *Generation of Swine*). [×4]

Misattribution warnings (do not use as quoted):
- The viral "**music** business is a cruel and shallow money trench... There's also a
  negative side" — subject swapped, punchline fabricated. [×4]
- "I hate to advocate drugs, alcohol, violence, or insanity..." — genuinely his
  spoken line, but page-citations to the Vegas novel are wrong; source as "spoken,
  various occasions." [×3]
- The "Wow! What a Ride!" poster quote — unverified as worded, likely apocryphal. [×2]

## 5. The attorney thread (the part this Desk's counsel will enjoy)
- Oscar Zeta Acosta: Chicano-movement lawyer in East L.A., author of *The
  Autobiography of a Brown Buffalo* (1972) and *The Revolt of the Cockroach People*
  (1973); Thompson's source-guide on the Salazar story and companion on the Vegas
  trips. [×4]
- Fictionalized as "Dr. Gonzo," the "300-pound Samoan attorney." Acosta's documented
  objection was not the on-page crimes but the erasure — being made Samoan instead of
  Chicano. [×3]
- The liability flap is a complete legal parable: Random House's lawyers feared libel
  over the character's crimes and wanted a release; the settlement put Acosta's name
  and photograph on the jacket — defusing libel by endorsement while destroying the
  disguise. "Of and concerning," resolved by consent. [×3]
- Acosta disappeared May–June 1974 near Mazatlán; never found. Thompson's valediction:
  "The Banshee Screams for Buffalo Meat" (RS, Dec 1977). [×4]
- The craft-side legal record: his longtime editor Robert Love's "A technical guide
  for editing gonzo" (Columbia Journalism Review, 2005) documents the idiosyncratic
  editing/fact-checking that made the work publishable — the institutional half of
  the fact-fiction rail. [×3]

## 6. Style mechanics per the critics (not our invention)
- Participatory narrator as measuring instrument — the through-line critics trace
  from *Hell's Angels* to the Derby piece (retrospectives; Mosser, "What's Gonzo
  about Gonzo Journalism?", IALJS). [×3]
- His own theory: gonzo rests on "Faulkner's idea that the best fiction is far more
  true than any kind of journalism"; "fiction is a bridge to the truth that
  journalism can't reach." [×2]
- Mankiewicz (McGovern's campaign director) on *Campaign Trail '72*: "the least
  factual, most accurate account" of the 1972 campaign — the canonical critical
  formula for why the method works. [×3]
- The wave speech is widely treated as the elegiac register's proof and his own
  favorite passage to read aloud. [×3]
- Steadman: "Our chemistry there made gonzo possible" — the ink is half the voice.
  [×4]
- The decline, said plainly by his biographer: McKeen (*Outlaw Journalist*, 2008) —
  "trapped in a mythology he had created," late work "descended into self-parody."
  Counterweight: Tom Wolfe called him the 20th century's greatest comic writer in
  English. Channel the peak; know the parabola. [×2–3]

## 7. Cautionary tales index (what the skill refuses to repeat)
- **The ibogaine affair** (*Campaign Trail '72*): he printed "a rumor" that Muskie was
  on ibogaine — a rumor he had started himself, then cited to itself; parts of the
  press took it half-seriously. His own words later: "I never said he was. I said
  there was a rumor in Milwaukee that he was, which was true when I started the
  rumor in Milwaukee." [×3] The deadpan register carried an invented fact into
  circulation: the exact failure mode rail 3 in legal-lines-for-counsel.md §3 exists to
  prevent.
- **The Acosta identification flap** (§5): the disguise you're sure is a disguise may
  not be one — "of and concerning" from inside the canon.
- **The self-mythology tax**: the torn-notebook origin and the etymologies of "gonzo"
  are self-told or spurious — hold the legends labeled, the way the founding legend
  deserves.
