# Research method — frame, map, search, read, check currency, stop

Contents: §1 Frame the question · §2 The authority hierarchy · §3 The search protocol ·
§4 Reading the authority · §5 Currency and subsequent history · §6 When to stop

## §1 Frame the question

Write these down before the first search; the ledger starts here.

| Item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Question presented** — *Under [jurisdiction] law, does [rule] apply when [facts]?* | A question without a jurisdiction cannot be answered; one without the operative facts is answered for someone else's case |
| **Posture** — transactional advice, litigation (which stage, which court), compliance | Decides which authority binds and how much certainty is needed |
| **Currency date** — "as of" when the answer must hold | A rule in flux (an agency position, a threshold) needs a date on the answer |
| **Facts that matter** — employer size, dates, state of work, contract terms, who did what when | Thresholds and elements turn on them; list what you do not yet know |
| **What would change the answer** — the fact or authority that flips it | Tells you when you can stop and what to flag as open |

## §2 The authority hierarchy

**Binding (mandatory) authority** — what the deciding court must follow — comes first, and the
memo says which authorities are binding and which are persuasive.

1. **Constitutions**: federal, then the state's.
2. **Statutes**: the official code (*uscode.house.gov*, *govinfo.gov*; the state legislature's
   site or official code). Read the definitions section and the effective-date provision.
3. **Regulations**: *ecfr.gov* for the current CFR, the *Federal Register* for amendments,
   proposed rules, vacaturs and withdrawals; the state administrative code. After *Loper Bright
   Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) federal courts no longer defer to an agency's reading of an
   ambiguous statute under *Chevron* [canon attribution]; an agency interpretation is persuasive to
   the extent it persuades, and the statute's text decides.
4. **Caselaw**: the highest court of the jurisdiction, then its intermediate appellate courts, then
   trial courts (not binding on other trial courts). Federal courts applying state law follow the
   state's highest court and predict it where it has not spoken (*Erie*) [canon attribution].
   Federal questions: the Supreme Court, then the circuit. Check the local rule on citing
   unpublished opinions.
5. **Persuasive authority**: other jurisdictions (weight by similarity of statute and reasoning),
   agency guidance and opinion letters (weight varies, and guidance is withdrawn without a case
   telling you), Restatements where courts in the jurisdiction have adopted them.
6. **Secondary sources**: treatises, practice guides, law reviews, ALR annotations. Orientation and
   finding aids. Cite them only where nothing primary exists, and say so.

**The federal–state seam.** Preemption (does federal law displace the state rule?), concurrent
schemes (employment law is layered: federal floor, state and local additions), and choice of law
in contracts (the governing-law clause, and the limits on it for employees and consumers).

## §3 The search protocol

Each search is logged: **database · query · date · what was reviewed**. The ledger is the memo's
audit trail and the next researcher's starting point.

1. **Orient** (only if the area is unfamiliar): a treatise chapter or practice guide to learn the
   vocabulary, the leading cases and the statute's structure. Do not cite from this step.
2. **Statute and regulation** from the official source. Read the whole section, the definitions,
   the effective date, and the notes on amendments. Search the Federal Register for the rule's
   docket to see proposed changes, stays, vacaturs.
3. **Caselaw — one good case.** Find one on-point decision from a binding court (from the
   treatise, the annotated code, or a search), then follow everything that cites it: CourtListener
   (*cited by* list), Google Scholar (*How cited*), Westlaw/Lexis if licensed. Then the most recent
   applications, with a date restriction.
4. **Terms and connectors** when the concept has stable vocabulary (`"reasonable accommodation" /s
   "undue hardship"`), natural-language search when it does not. Both, logged.
5. **Contrary authority.** Search for the opposite proposition. If there is none, the memo says
   you looked and where.
6. **Free and licensed sources.** CourtListener and Google Scholar hold most published opinions;
   court websites hold slip opinions; Westlaw, Lexis and Fastcase add editorial material and
   citators. The verifier in this skill checks existence against CourtListener; it does not replace
   a citator for currency (§5).

## §4 Reading the authority

- **Holding versus dicta.** The holding is what the court decided on the facts before it, on the
  issue it had to decide. Everything else is dicta, cite-able as persuasive and labelled as such.
- **Posture and standard of review.** A summary-judgment reversal, a motion-to-dismiss affirmance
  and a bench-trial finding say different things about the same words. Record the posture in the
  ledger line for the case.
- **Whose words.** A quotation may be the majority, a plurality, a concurrence, a dissent, the
  lower court, or a party's argument restated. Cite the majority; label anything else.
- **Pinpoint.** Every proposition gets the page it comes from. A cite without a pinpoint is a claim
  that the whole opinion supports the sentence, which is rarely true.
- **Does it actually support the proposition?** The most common error in careful work: a real case,
  correctly cited, that does not say what the sentence says. Read the pinpointed page with the
  sentence next to it.
- **Statutes.** Operative text, defined terms, exceptions, effective date, and whether the version
  you are reading is the one in force on the relevant date.

## §5 Currency and subsequent history

| Authority | Check | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Case | reversed, vacated, overruled, abrogated, superseded by statute, criticised, distinguished | citator (KeyCite, Shepard's) if licensed; CourtListener *cited by*; search the case name with *overruled* / *abrogated* / *superseded* / *reversed*; the later opinions themselves |
| Statute | amended, repealed, renumbered, pending bills, effective dates | official code history notes; the legislature's site; the session laws |
| Regulation | amended, stayed, vacated, withdrawn, enforcement paused | *ecfr.gov* (current text), the *Federal Register* docket, the agency's own announcements, the litigation vacating it |
| Agency guidance | rescinded, superseded, reissued | the agency's site and the Federal Register; guidance disappears without a case telling you |

**Two recent examples of a rule that looks current and is not** [snippet-only, cross-checked]:
the Federal Trade Commission's 2024 non-compete rule was set aside by a federal district court in
August 2024, the Commission dropped its appeals in September 2025, and the rule was removed from
the Code of Federal Regulations with effect from February 2026; the Department of Labor's 2024
overtime salary-threshold rule was vacated in November 2024 and formally rescinded in May 2026, so
the enforceable threshold reverted to the 2019 figure. A cached copy of either rule, or a 2024
article about it, reads as current law and is not. Verify the live source and date the answer.

## §6 When to stop

Research is sufficient when the memo can state, with a ledger line for each: the rule; the
binding authority for it; the most recent application in the jurisdiction; the contrary authority
or the search that found none; the currency check for each authority cited; and the facts the
answer turns on. Stop there. Additional cases that say the same thing add length, not confidence;
if more confidence is needed, say what would supply it (a fact, a filing, a citator report).
